Case study · Famous People

From pencil sketches to a party game people actually play.

An idea sketched on paper more than 15 years ago, made real with Claude. In four weeks it became a multiplayer game, a dashboard to run it, and a site to tell people about it.

9 August 2026· 4 minute read· Braden Turner, Pivot Dev Shop

Famous People is a name-guessing party game. Everyone joins from their own phone with a four-character code, and the game passes between phones instead of the phone passing between people.

Behind the game sits a private dashboard for curating the names and a marketing site — the kind of thing a small product team would normally build. This was one person working with Claude, in evenings and spare moments, mostly from an iPhone: describing what should change, looking at the result, and deciding what came next.

What got built

Three pieces, one product

The game

Play from your own phone

An installable web app. Host starts a game, players scan a QR code, everyone builds a secret list of names, then three rounds run across the group. Whichever phone is live drives the round — so the game survives the host's battery dying.

The admin console

Curate the deck

Approving names players submit mid-game, writing descriptions, organising categories, bulk edits. The difference between a demo and something you can actually run.

Where it started

The whole game, in pencil

Before any code, every screen existed as an idea — a vision of the experience — captured on these iPhone frames more than 15 years ago. Luckily I'd kept them, and Claude brought the idea back to life.

What's striking looking back isn't how rough these are — it's how little the structure changed. The screens that shipped are the screens that were drawn, because the cheapest thing in the world to argue with is a drawing. The photo-of-the-winners note is the exception that makes the point: finding out it wasn't worth building cost one pencil stroke.

Everything that did change came from watching people play. Hiding names someone else already took. Making the current player tap a card before their turn starts, so nobody's turn begins while they're still looking at their phone. Removing a Skip button that turned out to be a way to cheat. None of that is visible in a sketch — you only find it in a living room.

Where it ended up

And here's what shipped

The same eight screens, in the same order, running on a phone. Put them next to the drawings and the thing worth noticing is how rarely anything moved.

Turning a sketch into a real life application validated ideas. In the sketch, three names written into three boxes and "I need inspiration" was the escape hatch for when a player got stuck. What shipped is the other way round: browsing categories is the main road, and typing your own is the field at the bottom. Nobody decided that in a meeting — it's what happened once real people met a blank text box and stalled.

Two screens here were never drawn at all. Hosting, above — the sketch imagined joining but not the person being joined. And a "who's playing" step that exists for one reason: at a real game night, somebody always turns up without a phone, and the host has to be able to add them.

One small change

From a sentence to the screen

Most of the work looked like this: notice something during a game, describe it in a sentence, and see it fixed a few minutes later.

What I asked for, from my phone

"Add padding between the waiting-for-players lobby and the Continue button."

That's the whole brief. No mockup, no ticket, no spec.

Before
After

It's a tiny change, and that's the point. When small things are this easy to fix, nothing waits — no ticket, no sprint, no queue. The game got better a little at a time, straight from what people said at the table.

Building stopped being the bottleneck. Deciding what to build became the whole job.

What we took away

Three things worth knowing

A prototype can now be the real thing

The gap between a clickable mock and software people can use has narrowed enormously. That changes what's worth arguing about in a room: you can often settle it by building the thing in an evening and watching six people use it.

Watch real people use it

The sketches got the structure right. Everything that made the game good came from watching people play it — where they paused, where they got stuck, what they reached for.

Knowing what to build is the job

With building this cheap, the hard part is deciding what's worth making and being honest about whether it works. That was always the hard part — now there's less in the way of it.
